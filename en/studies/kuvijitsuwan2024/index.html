Bottom line: This paper must not be counted as direct SYREBO engineering research. It evaluates a university-built air-guitar measurement system; SYREBO appears only as a commercial-device example in the introduction. Its contribution concerns validity and test–retest reliability for static finger-flexion measurement, not SYREBO performance or efficacy.

Research design

The study enrolled ten healthy, right-handed, asymptomatic participants (mean age 34.6 years; eight men, two women). It compared air-guitar measurements with a Jamar finger goniometer and repeated testing in the morning and afternoon of the same day.

The air-guitar was developed at Chiang Mai University and is not a SYREBO product. It assessed static maximum flexion, with a system resolution of 0.01°, compared with typical 5° goniometer readings.

Key findings

  • Agreement with the goniometer yielded CCC values of 0.62–0.90, with mean differences of about 1°.
  • Air-guitar test–retest ICC values were 0.82–0.99; SEM was 1°–3° and MDC95 was 1°–9°.
  • These findings concern the university-built measurement system and do not validate a SYREBO device.

Why it matters

Including this paper in the appendix illustrates the difference between a secondary mention and a direct experiment. Brand-name searching can mistakenly capture introductions, discussions and reference lists; the actual device in the methods determines classification.

Evidence boundaries

  • Only ten healthy participants were included, limiting extrapolation to patients.
  • Only static maximum flexion was measured, without a 3D dynamic gold standard.
  • The experimental device was university-built; this is not direct SYREBO clinical or engineering research.
