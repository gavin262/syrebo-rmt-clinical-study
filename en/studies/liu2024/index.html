Brain Activation Pattern Caused by Soft Rehabilitation Glove and Virtual Reality Scenes: A Pilot fNIRS Study

IEEE Transactions on Neural Systems and Rehabilitation Engineering, 32, 3848–3857

DOI: 10.1109/TNSRE.2024.3482470 · Original record

Evidence scope: the PubMed abstract, PMID and bibliographic details were verified. Glove brand and model still require full-text methods verification and are not inferred from the topic alone.

fNIRS | RA / VR / VRA tasks | Not an efficacy trial

Bottom line: This is a brain-activation mechanism study, not a clinical functional-efficacy trial. Twenty healthy participants and 20 patients with stroke completed robotic-glove-assisted passive grasping, VR grasping-video observation, and combined glove-plus-VR tasks. VR and combined tasks activated broader cortical areas, but this does not establish functional recovery.

Research design

The three controlled tasks were robotic-glove-assisted passive grasping (RA), watching a grasping-movement video in virtual reality (VR), and combined robotic glove plus VR (VRA). fNIRS signals were recorded synchronously over prefrontal, motor and occipital regions to analyze activation and functional connectivity.

The research question concerns brain responses to different sensory conditions, not changes in FMA, ARAT or daily-living function after weeks of training. PMID: 39418155.

Key findings

  • Both groups showed activation of broader cortical areas during VR and VRA tasks.
  • The stroke group had higher average cortical activation than the healthy group across all three tasks, especially in prefrontal cortex (P<0.05 in the abstract).
  • Functional connectivity was weaker in the stroke group across most regions but significantly stronger in some right-hemisphere regions. The authors propose potential for multisensory stimulation to engage communication among more brain regions.

Why it matters

For product and rehabilitation-pathway discussions, the study offers mechanistic clues about how passive movement plus visual scenes alter brain activation. It supports further investigation of multisensory training, not direct promises of functional recovery or durable efficacy.

Evidence boundaries

  • This pilot mechanistic study did not use clinical functional recovery as its primary outcome.
  • Higher activation in patients may reflect compensation or other processes and should not be simplified to “more activation is better.”
  • Glove brand/model await full-text verification; the study is not counted here as a confirmed SYREBO-device experiment.
